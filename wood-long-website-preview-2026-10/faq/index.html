Child Custody

Does Ohio favor mothers in custody cases?

No. Under ORC 3109.04, Ohio courts must treat both parents as standing on equal footing when deciding custody. The judge looks at the child's best interest using factors set out in the statute, such as each parent's relationship with the child, the child's adjustment to home and school, and which parent is more likely to support the child's time with the other parent.

At what age can a child choose which parent to live with in Ohio?

There is no age at which an Ohio child gets to decide. A judge may interview the child privately in chambers and will consider the child's wishes as one best-interest factor, giving them more weight as the child matures. The final decision still belongs to the court, which weighs the child's wishes alongside every other factor in ORC 3109.04.

What is the difference between shared parenting and being the residential parent?

With a sole allocation, one parent is named residential parent and legal custodian and makes the major decisions, while the other parent has parenting time. With shared parenting, both parents keep decision-making rights and responsibilities under a court-approved plan. Shared parenting does not have to mean an equal split of time. The plan sets the schedule.

If we were never married, who has custody?

Under ORC 3109.042, an unmarried mother is the sole residential parent and legal custodian until a court issues an order that says otherwise. A father who wants custody or parenting time must first have paternity established and then ask the juvenile court for an order. Both parents stand on equal footing once that case is filed.

Child Support

How is child support calculated in Ohio?

Ohio uses an income shares model. The court or child support agency adds both parents' annual incomes, looks up the basic support obligation for that combined income and number of children on the state schedule, and divides it by each parent's share of the total. Adjustments are made for health insurance, child care, other children and parenting time. The result is the guideline amount.

When does child support end in Ohio?

Under ORC 3119.86, support generally continues until the child turns 18. If the child is still attending an accredited high school on a full-time basis, support continues until graduation but not past age 19, unless the order says otherwise. Support can continue longer for a child who cannot support themselves because of a mental or physical disability that began before age 18.

Can I stop paying child support if I don't get to see my child?

No. In Ohio, child support and parenting time are separate obligations. If you stop paying, you can build up arrears and face enforcement such as wage withholding or license suspension. If the other parent is denying your court-ordered parenting time, the right step is to file a motion asking the court to enforce the parenting time order.

How do I change my child support order?

You can ask the county child support enforcement agency for an administrative review or file a motion with the court that issued the order. Under ORC 3119.79, if a new guideline calculation is more than 10% higher or lower than the current amount, that difference counts as a substantial change of circumstances that supports a modification.

Dissolution

How long does a dissolution take in Ohio?

Once the petition is filed, Ohio law requires the court to hold the final hearing no sooner than 30 days and no later than 90 days after filing, under ORC 3105.64. The time before filing varies more. Reaching a full separation agreement and preparing the paperwork can take a few weeks or several months, depending on your finances, your children and how quickly you and your spouse agree.

Do we both have to go to the dissolution hearing?

Yes. Ohio law requires both spouses to appear at the final hearing. Each spouse tells the court, under oath, that they voluntarily signed the separation agreement, are satisfied with it, and want the marriage dissolved. If one spouse does not appear or no longer agrees, the court cannot grant the dissolution on that petition.

Can we use one lawyer for a dissolution?

A lawyer can represent only one spouse. The other spouse can hire their own lawyer or go without one. Even when you agree on everything, each spouse has separate interests in the property, support and parenting terms, so having your own lawyer review the separation agreement before you sign it helps you understand what you are agreeing to.

What if we start a divorce and then reach an agreement?

You can ask to convert the divorce to a dissolution. Under ORC 3105.08, at any time before a final divorce decree, the spouses may file a motion to convert the case and file the documents a dissolution requires, including a signed separation agreement. The court then treats the case as a dissolution and sets the hearing.

Divorce

Do I need a reason to get divorced in Ohio?

Yes, a divorce must rest on one of the grounds in ORC 3105.01, but you do not have to prove fault. Incompatibility is a ground unless your spouse denies it. Living separate and apart without cohabitation for one year is also a ground, and your spouse cannot block it by disagreeing. Fault grounds, such as adultery or extreme cruelty, still exist but are used less often.

How long do I have to live in Ohio before filing for divorce?

The person filing must have been an Ohio resident for at least six months right before filing, under ORC 3105.03. Separately, the case is normally filed in a county where one spouse has lived for at least 90 days. If you recently moved to Columbus from another Ohio county, you may need to file in the county you came from or wait.

What is the difference between a contested and an uncontested divorce?

An uncontested divorce is one where both spouses end up agreeing on every issue, so the court only needs to approve the terms. A contested divorce has at least one issue the spouses cannot settle, such as property, support or parenting time, and the judge or magistrate decides it after a trial. Many cases start contested and settle along the way.

Can my spouse stop me from getting a divorce in Ohio?

Not in the long run. Your spouse can deny incompatibility, which removes that one ground. But if you have lived separate and apart without cohabitation for one year, or another ground applies, the court can grant the divorce even if your spouse objects. A spouse who refuses to take part can still be served, and the case can proceed without them.

Grandparent Rights

Do grandparents have visitation rights in Ohio?

Not automatically. Ohio lets grandparents ask a court for companionship or visitation only in specific situations: during or after the parents' divorce, dissolution, legal separation, annulment or a child support case; after a parent has died; or when the child's mother was unmarried. Even then, the court must find visitation in the child's best interest and give special weight to the parents' wishes.

Can grandparents get visitation if the parents are married and living together?

Generally no. When the child's parents are married to each other, living together and no qualifying court case is pending, Ohio law does not give grandparents a way to file for visitation. The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Troxel v. Granville protects fit parents' right to decide who spends time with their children, and Ohio's statutes are written around that limit.

How can a grandparent get custody of a grandchild in Ohio?

A grandparent can ask the juvenile court for legal custody, but must generally show that each parent is unsuitable, for example through abandonment, inability to care for the child, or that parental custody would harm the child. In a divorce case, the court can also place a child with a relative if it finds custody with either parent is not in the child's best interest.

What is the difference between grandparent visitation and custody?

Visitation, which Ohio calls companionship or visitation, gives a grandparent scheduled time with the child while the parents keep custody and decision-making rights. Custody transfers the right to have the child live with you and to make major decisions. Because custody takes rights away from a parent, Ohio courts require a much stronger showing for custody than for visitation.

Guardian ad Litem

Who does a guardian ad litem represent in Ohio?

A guardian ad litem represents the child's best interest, not either parent. The GAL's job is to investigate and give the court an independent recommendation. That means the GAL may agree with you on some points and not others. A GAL is different from a lawyer for the child, although one person can sometimes serve in both roles if the child's wishes and best interest line up.

Does the judge have to follow the GAL's recommendation?

No. The GAL's report is one piece of evidence the court considers along with testimony, documents and the best-interest factors in Ohio law. Courts often give the report weight because the GAL has spoken with everyone involved, but the judge or magistrate makes the decision and can reach a different conclusion based on all of the evidence.

What can I do if I think the GAL got it wrong?

You generally have the right to question the GAL at the hearing about the investigation, the people interviewed and the basis for each recommendation. You can present your own evidence and witnesses. If a magistrate rules, you can file written objections with the judge, usually within 14 days. Courts also have a process for complaints about a GAL's conduct.

Who pays for a guardian ad litem?

In most private custody cases, the parents pay the GAL's fees. The court usually orders a deposit at the start and decides how the cost is split between the parents, often based on income or other factors. Ask the court or your lawyer how fees work in your case.

LGBTQ+ Family Law

Is divorce different for same-sex couples in Ohio?

The law is the same. Since the U.S. Supreme Court's 2015 decision in Obergefell v. Hodges, same-sex marriages are valid in Ohio and end through the same divorce or dissolution process as any marriage. The same rules apply to property division, spousal support, custody and child support. Some cases raise extra questions, especially about property from years before a couple could legally marry.

We were together long before we could marry. Does that time count?

It can matter, but Ohio law does not give a clear answer. Ohio generally counts the marriage from the wedding date, and common-law marriage has not been allowed in Ohio since 1991. Courts have some flexibility to use different dates when the usual dates would be unfair, and how that applies to couples who could not marry earlier is still developing. This is a fact-specific question worth discussing with a lawyer.

If both of us are on our child's birth certificate, do we still need an adoption?

Many family law attorneys recommend it. A birth certificate is a record, not a court judgment. An adoption decree is a court order that other states and countries must generally recognize. Because the law on parentage for same-sex parents is still developing in some places, an adoption can give the non-biological parent more secure legal standing, especially if you travel or move.

Should we sign a prenuptial agreement?

A prenuptial agreement can be especially useful if you built a life together before marrying, own property separately or together, or have children from earlier relationships. It lets you decide in advance how property and support will be handled. In Ohio, an agreement needs full financial disclosure and must be signed voluntarily, so start well before the wedding.

Mediation

Is what I say in mediation confidential in Ohio?

Generally, yes. Ohio's Uniform Mediation Act creates a privilege that keeps most mediation communications from being used as evidence in court. There are limited exceptions, such as threats of violence and information about child abuse or neglect that must be reported. A signed agreement that comes out of mediation is not confidential, since the point is to file it with the court.

Do I have to agree to anything in mediation?

No. The mediator does not decide your case or make rulings. You and the other party decide whether to reach an agreement, and you can stop if it is not working. Even when a court orders you to attend mediation, it cannot force you to settle. If you do not reach an agreement, the case goes back to the judge or magistrate.

Is mediation a good idea if there has been domestic violence?

Often it is not. Ohio courts screen for domestic violence before mediation, and mediation may not be used, or may only be used with safety measures, when there has been abuse. Mediation works well only when both people can speak freely and safely. If you have safety concerns, tell the court and the mediator, and ask about other options.

What is the difference between mediation and collaborative divorce?

In mediation, one neutral mediator helps both of you reach agreement, and each of you may also have your own lawyer. In collaborative divorce, each spouse hires a collaboratively trained lawyer, and everyone signs an agreement to settle without going to court. If the process breaks down, those lawyers withdraw and you would need new lawyers for court.

Paternity

Does signing the birth certificate make me the legal father in Ohio?

Signing an acknowledgment of paternity affidavit, which is often offered at the hospital, legally establishes paternity once it becomes final. Either signer can rescind it within 60 days of the last signature by requesting a genetic-testing determination through the child support agency. After that, it can only be challenged in court for fraud, duress or material mistake of fact, within one year.

If paternity is established, do I automatically get parenting time?

No. Under ORC 3109.042, an unmarried mother remains the sole residential parent and legal custodian until a court orders otherwise, even after paternity is established. A father must file in juvenile court for an order on custody, shared parenting or parenting time. Once he files, the court treats both parents equally and decides based on the child's best interest.

How do I get a DNA test to establish paternity in Ohio?

You can ask the county child support enforcement agency to arrange genetic testing, usually by cheek swab. The agency then issues an administrative order finding that a father-child relationship does or does not exist. You can also file a parentage action in juvenile court, and the court can order genetic testing as part of that case.

Is there a deadline to establish paternity in Ohio?

A parentage action under ORC Chapter 3111 can generally be brought until five years after the child turns 18, meaning up to the child's 23rd birthday. Child support issues, inheritance rights and the father's ability to seek parenting time all depend on paternity, so most families are better served by establishing it early.

Post-Decree Modification

Can I change a custody order in Ohio?

Yes, but the bar is meant to be high so children have stability. The court must find a change in circumstances since the last order and that the change would serve your child's best interest. When one parent is the residential parent, the court must also find that the benefits of changing outweigh the harm the change would cause, unless both parents agree or another exception applies.

When can child support be changed in Ohio?

You can ask for a review at any time, but the most common path is the 10 percent rule. If the support amount recalculated under Ohio's guidelines is more than 10 percent higher or lower than the current amount, Ohio law treats that as a substantial change. Changes in income, health insurance costs or child care costs often trigger this. Reviews can go through the court or the county child support agency.

Can spousal support be modified after an Ohio divorce?

Only if your decree says the court kept jurisdiction to change it. If it did, the person asking must show a substantial change in circumstances that makes the current award no longer reasonable and that was not considered when the order was made. If the decree does not reserve jurisdiction, the amount and length are generally final, even if circumstances change.

What can I do if my ex will not pay support or follow the parenting schedule?

You can file a motion asking the court to find the other parent in contempt. If the court agrees, it can order payment of what is owed, make-up parenting time, attorney fees, fines, or even jail time in some cases. For unpaid child support, the county child support enforcement agency can also use tools like wage withholding and license suspension.

Prenuptial Agreements

Are prenuptial agreements enforceable in Ohio?

Yes, if they meet the standards Ohio courts apply. The agreement must be in writing and signed, entered into freely without fraud, duress, coercion or overreaching, and made with full disclosure or full knowledge of the other person's assets. Its terms must not promote divorce. Spousal support terms can also be reviewed at the time of divorce to see whether they have become unconscionable because circumstances changed.

Can we sign a postnuptial agreement in Ohio?

Yes. Before 2023, Ohio law did not allow spouses to change their legal relations by contract during the marriage, except to agree to an immediate separation. An amendment to ORC 3103.06 and the new ORC 3103.061 now permit postnuptial agreements. They must be in writing, signed by both spouses, entered freely, based on full disclosure, and not unconscionable.

How far before the wedding should we sign a prenup?

Ohio law sets no fixed deadline, but earlier is better. When an agreement is presented shortly before the wedding, a court may look more closely at whether it was signed freely, especially if one person had no real chance to consult a lawyer. Starting the conversation and exchanging financial information a few months ahead gives both people time to review and negotiate.

Can a prenup decide child custody or child support?

No. Ohio courts decide custody, parenting time and child support based on the children's interests at the time of the divorce, and parents cannot give up a child's right to support in advance. A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can address property, debts and spousal support, but any terms about children are not binding on the court.

Property Division

Does Ohio split everything 50/50 in a divorce?

Ohio starts with an equal division of marital property, but not of everything. Under ORC 3105.171, separate property, such as assets owned before marriage, inheritances and gifts to one spouse, is generally returned to its owner. Marital property is divided equally unless the court finds an equal split would be inequitable, in which case it divides the property in a way it finds fair after weighing the statutory factors.

Is my inheritance marital property in Ohio?

An inheritance received by one spouse during the marriage is separate property under ORC 3105.171. It can lose that status if it is mixed with marital money so thoroughly that it can no longer be traced, for example, deposited in a joint account and spent on shared expenses. Keeping records that show where the money came from and where it went is the main way to protect it.

What is a QDRO?

A QDRO, or qualified domestic relations order, is a separate court order that tells a retirement plan administrator how to divide a 401(k), pension or similar plan between spouses. The divorce decree says how the account is divided; the QDRO carries it out. Without one, many plans will not pay the former spouse their share. Some plans, such as government pensions, use a differently named order.

What happens to our debts in an Ohio divorce?

The court divides marital debts along with marital assets, and the same equal-unless-inequitable rule applies. Keep in mind that the divorce decree binds you and your spouse, not your creditors. If both names are on a loan or card and your former spouse stops paying, the lender can still pursue you. Refinancing or closing joint accounts helps limit that risk.

Protection Orders

Can I get a protection order the same day I file in Ohio?

Often, yes. If you ask for an ex parte order when you file a domestic violence petition, Ohio law requires the court to hold a short hearing that same day. If the judge or magistrate finds it is needed, a temporary order can be issued right away. The other person does not need to be present for this first step. The order lasts until the full hearing.

How long does a civil protection order last in Ohio?

After a full hearing, a domestic violence civil protection order or a civil stalking protection order can last up to five years. The court sets the end date in the order itself. Before an order expires, the protected person can ask the court to renew it. A temporary ex parte order lasts only until the full hearing is held, unless the hearing is continued.

Is a protection order the same as a restraining order in a divorce?

No. A restraining order in a divorce is usually a standard order that keeps both spouses from hiding money, selling property or harassing each other while the case is open. A civil protection order is a separate case focused on safety. It can last years after a divorce ends, and breaking it is a crime, not just contempt of court.

I was served with a protection order petition. What should I do?

Read the order carefully and follow every term right away, even if you believe the petition is untrue. Do not contact the person who filed, even to apologize or explain. Write down the full hearing date and plan to attend. You have the right to present your side, bring evidence and question witnesses. Talking with a lawyer before the hearing can help you prepare.

Spousal Support

Is there a spousal support calculator in Ohio?

No. Unlike child support, Ohio has no statewide formula for spousal support. The court weighs the factors in ORC 3105.18, such as each spouse's income and earning ability, the length of the marriage and the standard of living, and decides what is appropriate and reasonable. Online calculators or rules of thumb you may see are not Ohio law and can be misleading for your situation.

How long does spousal support last in Ohio?

There is no fixed rule. The court sets a duration based on the ORC 3105.18 factors, and the length of the marriage weighs heavily. Shorter marriages often lead to shorter support, or none. In long marriages, or where one spouse has little ability to become self-supporting, support may last longer or have no set end date. Support ends when either spouse dies unless the order says otherwise.

Can spousal support be changed after the divorce?

Only if the decree allows it. Under ORC 3105.18(E), the court can modify spousal support only when the divorce decree or the dissolution separation agreement expressly keeps the court's power to do so. Even then, the person asking must show a substantial change in circumstances that was not taken into account when support was first set, such as a significant change in income.

Can I get support while the divorce is pending?

Yes. Under ORC 3105.18, the court can order reasonable temporary spousal support while a divorce or legal separation case is open. Temporary support helps cover living expenses until the final decree. It is decided early in the case, usually from income and expense information both spouses submit, and it does not lock in what the final award will be.

Stepparent Adoption

Can I adopt my stepchild without the other parent's consent in Ohio?

Sometimes. Under ORC 3107.07(A), consent is not required if the probate court finds that the other parent, without justifiable cause, failed to have more than minimal contact with the child or failed to provide required support for at least one year immediately before the petition was filed. You must prove this by clear and convincing evidence, and the other parent gets notice and a hearing.

Which court handles stepparent adoptions in Ohio?

Adoptions in Ohio are handled by the probate court, not the domestic relations or juvenile court. You generally file in the probate court of the county where you live. The probate court decides whether the other parent's consent is required, reviews any background checks or assessments, and holds the final hearing before granting the adoption.

Does a stepparent adoption end the other parent's child support?

A final adoption decree ends the other biological parent's parental rights and responsibilities, which includes the duty to pay child support going forward. The stepparent then takes on a full legal duty to support the child. Whether unpaid support from before the adoption can still be collected is a separate question to raise with a lawyer before you file.

Does my stepchild have to agree to the adoption?

If the child is over 12, Ohio generally requires the child's consent to the adoption under ORC 3107.06, unless the court finds that requiring it is not in the child's best interest. Younger children do not give formal consent, but the court may still consider their views when deciding whether the adoption is in their best interest.
