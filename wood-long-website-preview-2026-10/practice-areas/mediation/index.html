Mediation

Family mediation is a private meeting where a neutral mediator helps you and the other party reach your own agreement on parenting, support or property. In Ohio, what is said in mediation is generally confidential under the Uniform Mediation Act. Mediation is voluntary in its outcome: nothing is final unless both sides agree and the court approves it.

Updated October 9, 2026.

How does family mediation work in Ohio?

Mediation is a meeting, or a series of meetings, where a neutral mediator helps you and the other party talk through the issues and try to reach an agreement. The mediator does not take sides, give either of you legal advice, or decide the case. You make the decisions.

Mediation can cover almost any family law issue: a parenting schedule, decision-making for your children, child support, spousal support, and dividing property and debt. Sessions are usually a few hours long. Some mediators meet with everyone in one room. Others use separate rooms and move between them, which is sometimes called shuttle mediation. Many sessions now happen by video.

If you reach an agreement, the mediator usually writes up a memorandum of the terms. Lawyers then turn it into the documents filed with the court, such as a separation agreement for a dissolution or a shared parenting plan. The agreement becomes binding when the court approves it.

Is mediation confidential?

Yes, with some limits. Ohio adopted the Uniform Mediation Act in ORC Chapter 2710. It creates a privilege that generally keeps what is said in mediation from being used as evidence in a later court case. The mediator generally cannot be called to testify about the mediation.

The privilege has exceptions. They include threats to cause bodily harm, information about child abuse or neglect, and a signed written agreement. Because mediation is confidential, you can explore options and ideas without worrying that an offer you floated will be used against you later.

What is the difference between court-ordered and private mediation?

Court-ordered mediation happens when a judge or magistrate refers your case to mediation, often for parenting issues. ORC 3109.052 allows courts to order mediation in cases about the allocation of parental rights. Many courts, including in Franklin County, have local rules or programs for this.

Private mediation is something you and the other party choose and pay for, usually with a mediator you select together. It can happen before any case is filed, during a case, or after a decree when something needs to change. Private mediation often moves faster because you set the schedule.

In both kinds, attending is one thing and agreeing is another. A court can require you to attend, but it cannot require you to settle.

When is mediation not appropriate?

Mediation may not be appropriate when there has been domestic violence or when one person cannot safely speak for themselves. Ohio courts and mediators are expected to screen for domestic violence before and during mediation.

Under ORC 3109.052, a court generally may not order mediation of parenting issues when a parent has been convicted of certain domestic violence offenses or a child has been found abused or neglected, unless the court finds in writing that mediation is in the best interest of the family. If mediation goes forward despite concerns, safety steps may include separate rooms, separate arrival times, or a support person.

Mediation can also be hard when one person is hiding information or will not engage in good faith. If you have safety concerns, see our page on protection orders and raise them with the court.

What is collaborative divorce?

Collaborative divorce is a separate process where each spouse hires a collaboratively trained lawyer and everyone agrees in writing to resolve the case without going to court. Ohio has a Collaborative Family Law Act in ORC Chapter 3105. The team may include a neutral financial professional or a mental health professional to help with parenting.

If the collaborative process breaks down, the collaborative lawyers withdraw, and each spouse must hire a new lawyer to go to court. That rule gives everyone a strong reason to keep working toward agreement.

Common questions

Is what I say in mediation confidential in Ohio?

Generally, yes. Ohio's Uniform Mediation Act creates a privilege that keeps most mediation communications from being used as evidence in court. There are limited exceptions, such as threats of violence and information about child abuse or neglect that must be reported. A signed agreement that comes out of mediation is not confidential, since the point is to file it with the court.

Do I have to agree to anything in mediation?

No. The mediator does not decide your case or make rulings. You and the other party decide whether to reach an agreement, and you can stop if it is not working. Even when a court orders you to attend mediation, it cannot force you to settle. If you do not reach an agreement, the case goes back to the judge or magistrate.

Is mediation a good idea if there has been domestic violence?

Often it is not. Ohio courts screen for domestic violence before mediation, and mediation may not be used, or may only be used with safety measures, when there has been abuse. Mediation works well only when both people can speak freely and safely. If you have safety concerns, tell the court and the mediator, and ask about other options.

What is the difference between mediation and collaborative divorce?

In mediation, one neutral mediator helps both of you reach agreement, and each of you may also have your own lawyer. In collaborative divorce, each spouse hires a collaboratively trained lawyer, and everyone signs an agreement to settle without going to court. If the process breaks down, those lawyers withdraw and you would need new lawyers for court.
