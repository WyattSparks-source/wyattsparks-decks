LGBTQ+ Family Law

Since Obergefell v. Hodges in 2015, same-sex couples in Ohio marry and divorce under the same laws as any other couple. Some issues need extra care, like dividing property for couples who were together before they could legally marry. Many LGBTQ+ parents also take steps such as adoption to make sure both parents' legal ties to their children are secure.

Updated October 9, 2026.

How does divorce work for same-sex couples in Ohio?

Same-sex couples divorce under the same Ohio laws as every other married couple. The U.S. Supreme Court’s 2015 decision in Obergefell v. Hodges, a case that began in Ohio, held that states must allow and recognize same-sex marriages. Marriages entered in Ohio or in another state are valid here.

You can end a marriage through a contested divorce or, if you agree on everything, a dissolution. In Franklin County, both are handled by the Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations. The same rules apply to property division, spousal support, custody, and child support.

What if we were together before we could legally marry?

Your relationship before marriage may matter, but Ohio does not have a clear rule for it yet. Under ORC 3105.171, marital property is generally what was acquired “during the marriage,” which usually runs from the wedding date to the final hearing. Ohio abolished common-law marriage as of October 10, 1991, under ORC 3105.12, so a long relationship before that does not create a marriage by itself.

The statute lets a court choose different dates for the marriage when using the usual dates would be unfair. How courts apply that to couples who were legally barred from marrying earlier is not settled in Ohio.

This can affect a house bought before the wedding, retirement savings built up over a long relationship, and spousal support, since the length of the marriage is one factor in support. Some couples have other legal tools available too, such as contract or property claims, depending on how assets were titled and paid for.

How can we secure both parents’ legal ties to our children?

Adoption is the most secure way for a non-biological parent to become a legal parent. If you are married, the non-biological spouse can usually file a stepparent adoption in Probate Court, which in Franklin County is the Franklin County Probate Court. Our stepparent adoption page explains that process.

When a child is born to a married couple, both spouses are commonly listed on the birth certificate. But a birth certificate is a record, not a court judgment. An adoption decree is a court order that other states and countries must generally honor. Many LGBTQ+ parents complete an adoption even when both names are already on the birth certificate, especially if they plan to travel or move.

For unmarried couples, adoption options in Ohio are more limited. Shared custody agreements through the Juvenile Court may be an option in some situations.

What about children born through assisted reproduction?

Ohio has statutes on donor insemination, but they were written with opposite-sex married couples in mind. They state that a husband who consents to his wife’s insemination with donor sperm is treated as the child’s legal father, and that the donor is not. Ohio law on surrogacy is also limited.

Because these rules do not clearly address every family, it helps to keep written consent forms and clinic records, and to plan for an adoption or other court judgment. If a relationship ends, these documents can matter a great deal in a custody case.

Do we need a prenuptial agreement?

A prenuptial agreement is worth considering, especially if you built a life together before marrying. It lets you decide ahead of time how property and support will be handled, including assets from your years together before the wedding. Ohio courts generally enforce prenuptial agreements when both people made full financial disclosure, signed freely without pressure, and the terms are not unconscionable.

If you are already married, Ohio has allowed postnuptial agreements since 2023 under ORC 3103.061. Learn more on our prenuptial agreements page.

Common questions

Is divorce different for same-sex couples in Ohio?

The law is the same. Since the U.S. Supreme Court's 2015 decision in Obergefell v. Hodges, same-sex marriages are valid in Ohio and end through the same divorce or dissolution process as any marriage. The same rules apply to property division, spousal support, custody and child support. Some cases raise extra questions, especially about property from years before a couple could legally marry.

We were together long before we could marry. Does that time count?

It can matter, but Ohio law does not give a clear answer. Ohio generally counts the marriage from the wedding date, and common-law marriage has not been allowed in Ohio since 1991. Courts have some flexibility to use different dates when the usual dates would be unfair, and how that applies to couples who could not marry earlier is still developing. This is a fact-specific question worth discussing with a lawyer.

If both of us are on our child's birth certificate, do we still need an adoption?

Many family law attorneys recommend it. A birth certificate is a record, not a court judgment. An adoption decree is a court order that other states and countries must generally recognize. Because the law on parentage for same-sex parents is still developing in some places, an adoption can give the non-biological parent more secure legal standing, especially if you travel or move.

Should we sign a prenuptial agreement?

A prenuptial agreement can be especially useful if you built a life together before marrying, own property separately or together, or have children from earlier relationships. It lets you decide in advance how property and support will be handled. In Ohio, an agreement needs full financial disclosure and must be signed voluntarily, so start well before the wedding.
