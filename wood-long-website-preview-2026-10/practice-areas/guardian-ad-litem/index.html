Guardian ad Litem

A guardian ad litem (GAL) is a person the court appoints in a custody case to look into the facts and recommend what is in the child's best interest. In Ohio, a GAL does not represent either parent. The judge or magistrate makes the final decision, and parents can challenge a GAL's report through questioning at the hearing and objections.

Updated October 9, 2026.

What is a guardian ad litem in an Ohio custody case?

A guardian ad litem, or GAL, is a person the court appoints to investigate and recommend what is in a child’s best interest. GALs are often attorneys, though some courts also use trained volunteers. A GAL may be appointed in a divorce, a custody case between unmarried parents, a post-decree change, or a grandparent visitation case.

A court may appoint a GAL on its own or because a parent asks for one. In Franklin County, that could be the Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations, or the Juvenile Court, depending on the case. ORC 3109.04 gives courts authority to appoint a GAL in custody matters. The Supreme Court of Ohio sets training and conduct standards for GALs in Rule 48 of the Rules of Superintendence.

What does a GAL actually do?

A GAL gathers facts, talks with the people involved, and gives the court a recommendation. Typical steps include:

  • Meeting with each parent, often more than once
  • Meeting with the child, sometimes at each parent’s home
  • Visiting each home
  • Talking with teachers, doctors, counselors, and other people who know the child
  • Reviewing school, medical, and court records

The GAL then writes a report for the court that explains what was done and gives a recommendation. Under the Supreme Court’s rules, the final report must generally be filed at least seven days before the final hearing, so parents have time to review it. The GAL may also testify at the hearing.

Does the GAL represent me or my child?

The GAL does not represent either parent. A GAL represents the child’s best interest, which is not always the same as what the child wants. A child’s own wishes are one factor, and the GAL will usually tell the court what the child wants even if the recommendation is different.

Because the GAL is neutral, what you say to the GAL is not confidential. The GAL may share it with the court and include it in the report.

How should I work with a GAL?

Treat the GAL as a neutral fact-finder and be cooperative, honest, and focused on your child. Some practical steps:

  • Respond to calls and emails promptly and keep scheduled appointments
  • Pay any required deposit on time
  • Give the GAL the names and contact information of people who know your child well
  • Share relevant documents, like school reports or medical records
  • Talk about your child’s needs, not complaints about the other parent
  • Do not coach your child about what to say

If you have a real safety concern, tell the GAL clearly and provide whatever records support it.

What can I do if I think the GAL got it wrong?

You have real options, and the GAL’s recommendation is not the final word. The judge or magistrate makes the decision based on all of the evidence.

Review the report early. Read it as soon as it is filed and note any facts that are wrong or left out.

Question the GAL at the hearing. Parents generally have the right to cross-examine the GAL about the investigation, who was and was not interviewed, and the reasons for each recommendation.

Present your own evidence. You can call witnesses and offer documents that tell a fuller story. In some cases, a separate custody evaluation may be appropriate.

File objections. If a magistrate decides the case, you can file written objections asking the judge to review the decision, usually within 14 days under Ohio Civil Rule 53. A transcript of the hearing is often needed.

Raise conduct concerns. If the issue is how the GAL did the job, such as not meeting a parent or missing deadlines, courts have a process for written comments or complaints about GALs. A complaint is separate from your case and does not by itself change the recommendation.

Common questions

Who does a guardian ad litem represent in Ohio?

A guardian ad litem represents the child's best interest, not either parent. The GAL's job is to investigate and give the court an independent recommendation. That means the GAL may agree with you on some points and not others. A GAL is different from a lawyer for the child, although one person can sometimes serve in both roles if the child's wishes and best interest line up.

Does the judge have to follow the GAL's recommendation?

No. The GAL's report is one piece of evidence the court considers along with testimony, documents and the best-interest factors in Ohio law. Courts often give the report weight because the GAL has spoken with everyone involved, but the judge or magistrate makes the decision and can reach a different conclusion based on all of the evidence.

What can I do if I think the GAL got it wrong?

You generally have the right to question the GAL at the hearing about the investigation, the people interviewed and the basis for each recommendation. You can present your own evidence and witnesses. If a magistrate rules, you can file written objections with the judge, usually within 14 days. Courts also have a process for complaints about a GAL's conduct.

Who pays for a guardian ad litem?

In most private custody cases, the parents pay the GAL's fees. The court usually orders a deposit at the start and decides how the cost is split between the parents, often based on income or other factors. Ask the court or your lawyer how fees work in your case.
