Ohio family law

Divorce

A divorce ends an Ohio marriage through a court case filed by one spouse in the county Domestic Relations court. You must have lived in Ohio for at least six months, and you must show a legal ground, such as incompatibility or living apart for a year. The court then divides property and debts and decides support and parenting if the spouses cannot agree.

Updated October 9, 2026.

What are the grounds for divorce in Ohio?

Ohio requires a legal reason for a divorce, and ORC 3105.01 lists them. Most people today rely on one of two no-fault grounds:

  • Incompatibility, unless your spouse denies it.
  • Living separate and apart without cohabitation for one year, without interruption. Your spouse cannot defeat this ground by disagreeing.

The statute also lists fault grounds: adultery, extreme cruelty, willful absence for one year, gross neglect of duty, habitual drunkenness, fraudulent contract, a spouse being in prison when the case is filed, a spouse already married to someone else at the time of the marriage, and a divorce obtained in another state that does not release the Ohio spouse. Fault can matter to the facts of a case, but in most Ohio divorces it does not change how property is divided.

Who can file for divorce in Ohio?

You can file if you have been an Ohio resident for at least six months immediately before filing, as set out in ORC 3105.03. The case is usually filed in the county where you or your spouse has lived for at least 90 days. In Columbus, that means the Franklin County Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations and Juvenile Branch.

What is the difference between a contested and an uncontested divorce?

An uncontested divorce is one where you and your spouse reach a full agreement, so the court reviews and approves it. A contested divorce is one where at least one issue has to be decided by a magistrate or judge.

The label often changes during a case. Many divorces are filed as contested and settle through negotiation or mediation before trial. If you and your spouse already agree on everything before anyone files, a dissolution is often a simpler path.

What happens during an Ohio divorce?

A divorce generally follows these steps:

  1. Filing. One spouse files a complaint for divorce with the Domestic Relations court, along with required financial and, if there are children, parenting forms.
  2. Service. The other spouse is formally served and has 28 days to file an answer. They may also file a counterclaim.
  3. Temporary orders. Either spouse can ask the court for orders that apply while the case is open (see below).
  4. Disclosure and discovery. Both sides exchange financial information, such as pay records, account statements, retirement balances and debts. In contested cases this can include formal requests, subpoenas and depositions.
  5. Negotiation, mediation and pretrial hearings. The court sets conferences to narrow the issues. Most cases settle at this stage.
  6. Trial, if needed. A magistrate or judge hears evidence and decides the open issues.
  7. Final decree. The court signs a decree of divorce that ends the marriage and sets out the division of property, any spousal support, and parenting and child support orders.

How long this takes depends on how much you and your spouse disagree about, how complex your finances are, and the court’s schedule. A case with children, a business or contested custody usually takes longer.

What are temporary orders in a divorce?

Temporary orders are short-term court orders that apply while the divorce is pending. Ohio law allows the court to order temporary spousal support during the case under ORC 3105.18, and the court can also make temporary orders on child support, parenting time, who stays in the home, and who pays which bills.

Courts also commonly issue restraining orders that stop either spouse from hiding, selling or running down marital assets while the case is open. Temporary orders do not decide the final outcome, but they shape daily life for months, so they deserve careful attention.

What is legal separation in Ohio?

Legal separation is a court case that sets property, support and parenting terms without ending the marriage. It is governed by ORC 3105.17 and uses the same grounds as divorce.

Some couples choose legal separation for religious reasons, to keep health insurance coverage, or because they are not ready to end the marriage. Neither spouse can remarry while legally separated. A legal separation can later be followed by a divorce if one spouse decides to end the marriage.

Common questions

Do I need a reason to get divorced in Ohio?

Yes, a divorce must rest on one of the grounds in ORC 3105.01, but you do not have to prove fault. Incompatibility is a ground unless your spouse denies it. Living separate and apart without cohabitation for one year is also a ground, and your spouse cannot block it by disagreeing. Fault grounds, such as adultery or extreme cruelty, still exist but are used less often.

How long do I have to live in Ohio before filing for divorce?

The person filing must have been an Ohio resident for at least six months right before filing, under ORC 3105.03. Separately, the case is normally filed in a county where one spouse has lived for at least 90 days. If you recently moved to Columbus from another Ohio county, you may need to file in the county you came from or wait.

What is the difference between a contested and an uncontested divorce?

An uncontested divorce is one where both spouses end up agreeing on every issue, so the court only needs to approve the terms. A contested divorce has at least one issue the spouses cannot settle, such as property, support or parenting time, and the judge or magistrate decides it after a trial. Many cases start contested and settle along the way.

Can my spouse stop me from getting a divorce in Ohio?

Not in the long run. Your spouse can deny incompatibility, which removes that one ground. But if you have lived separate and apart without cohabitation for one year, or another ground applies, the court can grant the divorce even if your spouse objects. A spouse who refuses to take part can still be served, and the case can proceed without them.
