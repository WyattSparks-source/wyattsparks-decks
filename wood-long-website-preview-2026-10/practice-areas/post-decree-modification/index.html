Ohio family law

Post-Decree Modification

After a divorce or custody case ends, Ohio courts can still change some orders when circumstances change. Custody changes require a change in circumstances and a finding that the change serves the child's best interest. Child support can be recalculated, and spousal support can be changed only if the court kept the power to do so.

Updated October 9, 2026.

Can a divorce or custody order be changed after it is final?

Yes, some parts can. Orders about children, including custody, parenting time, and child support, can be changed when circumstances change, because the court keeps authority over children until they are adults. Spousal support can be changed only if the decree says so. Property division is generally final and is not modified later.

In Franklin County, requests to change a divorce or dissolution decree go to the Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations. If your original case involved unmarried parents, it is usually handled in the Juvenile Court that made the order. A request to change an order is called a motion, and the other side must be served with it.

What does it take to change custody in Ohio?

You must show a change in circumstances and that changing custody is in your child’s best interest. These rules are in ORC 3109.04(E). The change must be based on facts that came up after the last order, or facts the court did not know about then. It must involve the child, the residential parent, or either parent under a shared parenting decree.

When one parent is the residential parent, the court should keep that parent in place unless one of these is true:

  • Both parents agree to the change
  • The child has been integrated into the other parent’s family with the residential parent’s consent
  • The harm likely caused by the change is outweighed by its benefits to the child

Changes to the terms of a shared parenting plan, like the schedule, follow a best-interest standard. For more on how the court looks at custody, see our child custody page.

When can child support be modified?

Child support can be modified when the recalculated amount would be more than 10 percent different from the current order. Under ORC 3119.79, a difference of more than 10 percent is treated as a change substantial enough to justify a new order. A new job, lost income, a change in health insurance, or new child care costs can each make the difference.

You can ask the court for a change, or you can ask the county child support enforcement agency for an administrative review. Changes are usually not retroactive to before the request was filed, so it helps to act soon after your situation changes. Read more on our child support page.

Can spousal support be changed?

Spousal support can be changed only if the court kept jurisdiction to change it. Under ORC 3105.18, the decree must expressly reserve that power. In a dissolution, the separation agreement must allow it.

If jurisdiction was kept, the person asking must show a substantial change in circumstances, such as a change in income or expenses, that makes the existing award no longer reasonable and appropriate. The change also must not have been taken into account when the original order was made. If your decree does not reserve jurisdiction, the terms are generally fixed. Our spousal support page explains more.

What if the other parent will not pay or follow the parenting schedule?

You can ask the court to enforce the order through a contempt motion. Contempt means a person has not followed a court order. ORC 2705.031 allows contempt actions for unpaid support and for interference with parenting time.

If the court finds contempt, it can order payment of what is owed, make-up parenting time, payment of attorney fees, fines, or in some cases jail time, which is often suspended if the person follows the order going forward. For unpaid child support, the county child support enforcement agency can also use wage withholding, tax refund intercepts, and license suspension.

What if a parent wants to move?

A residential parent who plans to move must file a notice of intent to relocate with the court. Under ORC 3109.051(G), the court sends a copy to the other parent, and either parent can ask for a hearing on whether the parenting schedule should change. Many Ohio decrees and local court rules add their own notice terms, so check your order. A move can also be a change in circumstances that supports a custody request.

Common questions

Can I change a custody order in Ohio?

Yes, but the bar is meant to be high so children have stability. The court must find a change in circumstances since the last order and that the change would serve your child's best interest. When one parent is the residential parent, the court must also find that the benefits of changing outweigh the harm the change would cause, unless both parents agree or another exception applies.

When can child support be changed in Ohio?

You can ask for a review at any time, but the most common path is the 10 percent rule. If the support amount recalculated under Ohio's guidelines is more than 10 percent higher or lower than the current amount, Ohio law treats that as a substantial change. Changes in income, health insurance costs or child care costs often trigger this. Reviews can go through the court or the county child support agency.

Can spousal support be modified after an Ohio divorce?

Only if your decree says the court kept jurisdiction to change it. If it did, the person asking must show a substantial change in circumstances that makes the current award no longer reasonable and that was not considered when the order was made. If the decree does not reserve jurisdiction, the amount and length are generally final, even if circumstances change.

What can I do if my ex will not pay support or follow the parenting schedule?

You can file a motion asking the court to find the other parent in contempt. If the court agrees, it can order payment of what is owed, make-up parenting time, attorney fees, fines, or even jail time in some cases. For unpaid child support, the county child support enforcement agency can also use tools like wage withholding and license suspension.
