Ohio family law

Grandparent Rights

Ohio grandparents do not have an automatic right to see their grandchildren. They can ask a court for companionship or visitation only in certain situations set by ORC 3109.051, 3109.11 and 3109.12, and the court must give special weight to the parents' wishes. Seeking custody is harder and generally requires showing that the parents are unsuitable.

Updated October 9, 2026.

Do grandparents have legal rights to see their grandchildren in Ohio?

Not automatically. Ohio law gives grandparents a way to ask a court for companionship or visitation in certain situations, but no right to visitation just because they are grandparents. Parents who are fit generally get to decide who spends time with their children.

That limit comes from the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Troxel v. Granville, 530 U.S. 57 (2000). The Court held that a fit parent’s decisions about the child’s relationships deserve special weight, and that a judge cannot override those decisions simply because the judge thinks a different arrangement would be better. The Supreme Court of Ohio later upheld Ohio’s grandparent visitation statutes in Harrold v. Collier (2005), because they require courts to give special weight to the parents’ wishes.

When can a grandparent file for visitation in Ohio?

Ohio’s statutes allow grandparents and other relatives to ask for companionship or visitation in three main situations:

  • The parents’ divorce, dissolution, legal separation, annulment or child support case. Under ORC 3109.051, a grandparent or other relative can file a motion for companionship or visitation in that case, during it or after the decree.
  • A parent has died. Under ORC 3109.11, the parents and other relatives of a deceased parent can ask the court for companionship or visitation.
  • The child’s mother was unmarried. Under ORC 3109.12, the mother’s parents and relatives can ask for visitation. The father’s parents and relatives can ask too, but only once paternity has been established.

If the child’s parents are married to each other and living together, and none of these situations applies, Ohio generally gives grandparents no way to file for visitation.

How do courts decide grandparent visitation?

The court must find that visitation is in the child’s best interest, using the factors in ORC 3109.051(D). Those include:

  • the child’s prior relationship with the grandparent
  • where the grandparent lives and how far it is from the child’s home
  • the child’s age, schedule and adjustment to home, school and community
  • the child’s wishes, if the judge interviews the child
  • the health and safety of the child
  • the wishes and concerns of the parents

Because of Troxel, the parents’ wishes are not just one factor among many. The court has to give them special weight. Grandparents who have had a close, ongoing role in the child’s life, such as regular care or shared holidays over many years, are in a stronger position than those who have had limited contact. A court may appoint a guardian ad litem to look into the family’s situation.

Can a grandparent get custody instead of visitation?

Yes, but the standard is much higher. Custody takes rights away from a parent, so Ohio courts protect parents’ rights closely.

  • Legal custody in juvenile court. A grandparent can ask the juvenile court for legal custody under ORC 2151.23. In a dispute between a parent and a non-parent, the court generally must first find the parent unsuitable. Under In re Perales (Ohio 1977), that means abandonment, giving up custody by agreement, being unable to care for the child, or that parental custody would be harmful to the child.
  • Placement in a divorce case. Under ORC 3109.04, if the court finds that custody with either parent is not in the child’s best interest, it can place the child with a relative.

Grandparents who are already raising a grandchild with the parent’s consent may also use a power of attorney or a caretaker authorization affidavit under Ohio law, which lets them enroll the child in school and consent to medical care without a custody order. These tools do not take rights away from the parent, and a parent can revoke them.

See our child custody page for more on how Ohio allocates parental rights.

What happens to grandparent visitation if the child is adopted?

An adoption can end a court-ordered visitation right in many situations, including a stepparent adoption. If an adoption is pending, it is worth getting advice early.

Common questions

Do grandparents have visitation rights in Ohio?

Not automatically. Ohio lets grandparents ask a court for companionship or visitation only in specific situations: during or after the parents' divorce, dissolution, legal separation, annulment or a child support case; after a parent has died; or when the child's mother was unmarried. Even then, the court must find visitation in the child's best interest and give special weight to the parents' wishes.

Can grandparents get visitation if the parents are married and living together?

Generally no. When the child's parents are married to each other, living together and no qualifying court case is pending, Ohio law does not give grandparents a way to file for visitation. The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Troxel v. Granville protects fit parents' right to decide who spends time with their children, and Ohio's statutes are written around that limit.

How can a grandparent get custody of a grandchild in Ohio?

A grandparent can ask the juvenile court for legal custody, but must generally show that each parent is unsuitable, for example through abandonment, inability to care for the child, or that parental custody would harm the child. In a divorce case, the court can also place a child with a relative if it finds custody with either parent is not in the child's best interest.

What is the difference between grandparent visitation and custody?

Visitation, which Ohio calls companionship or visitation, gives a grandparent scheduled time with the child while the parents keep custody and decision-making rights. Custody transfers the right to have the child live with you and to make major decisions. Because custody takes rights away from a parent, Ohio courts require a much stronger showing for custody than for visitation.
