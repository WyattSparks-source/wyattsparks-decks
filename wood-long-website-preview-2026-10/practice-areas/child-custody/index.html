Ohio family law

Child Custody

Ohio law calls custody the "allocation of parental rights and responsibilities." A court either names one parent the residential parent and legal custodian or approves a shared parenting plan, and every decision turns on the child's best interest under ORC 3109.04.

Updated October 9, 2026.

What does “custody” mean under Ohio law?

Ohio statutes don’t use the word “custody” much. The legal term is the allocation of parental rights and responsibilities, and it covers two things: where the child lives and who makes the major decisions about school, health care and religion.

A court can allocate those rights in one of two ways under ORC 3109.04:

  • Sole allocation. One parent is named the residential parent and legal custodian. That parent makes the major decisions. The other parent usually has parenting time, which is Ohio’s term for what other states call visitation.
  • Shared parenting. Both parents keep parental rights and responsibilities under a court-approved shared parenting plan. Shared parenting describes decision-making. It does not require a 50/50 schedule.

How do Ohio courts decide what is in the child’s best interest?

The judge applies the best-interest factors listed in ORC 3109.04(F)(1). They include:

  • each parent’s wishes, and the child’s wishes if the judge interviews the child in chambers
  • the child’s relationships with parents, siblings and other important people
  • the child’s adjustment to home, school and community
  • the mental and physical health of everyone involved
  • which parent is more likely to honor court-ordered parenting time
  • whether either parent has failed to pay court-ordered child support
  • any history of abuse, neglect or domestic violence
  • whether a parent has kept the other parent from court-ordered time with the child
  • whether a parent plans to move out of Ohio

No single factor decides a case. The court looks at the whole picture. Both parents start out equal, no matter their gender.

How does a shared parenting plan work?

If you want shared parenting, you or the other parent must file a proposed shared parenting plan with the court. If both of you agree on one plan, the court reviews it and approves it if it is in the child’s best interest. If you each file a different plan, the court can approve one, ask for changes, or reject both. A plan must be filed at least 30 days before the hearing on parental rights.

Before it approves shared parenting, the court also looks at the extra factors in ORC 3109.04(F)(2). These include how well the parents can cooperate and make decisions together, how close their homes are to each other, any history of abuse, and the guardian ad litem’s recommendation if one was appointed.

A good plan covers the weekly schedule, holidays and school breaks, transportation, how you will make decisions, how you will communicate, and what happens if one parent wants to move.

Which court handles custody in Ohio?

The answer depends on whether the parents are married.

  • Married parents. Custody is decided as part of the divorce, dissolution or legal separation case in the domestic relations court. See our divorce and dissolution pages.
  • Unmarried parents. Custody and parenting time are decided in juvenile court under ORC 2151.23.

If you were never married, there is an important default rule. Under ORC 3109.042, the mother is the sole residential parent and legal custodian until a court orders otherwise. A father who wants custody or parenting time needs legal paternity first, then a separate juvenile court order. Once that case is filed, the court treats both parents as equals.

What does a guardian ad litem do in a custody case?

A guardian ad litem (GAL) is a person the court appoints to look into the family’s situation and recommend what is best for the child. A GAL may meet with each parent, visit both homes, talk with the child, and review school and medical records. The GAL then files a report with the court.

The judge does not have to follow the GAL’s recommendation, but it often carries real weight. Learn more on our guardian ad litem page.

Can a custody order be changed later?

Yes, but not easily. To change who is the residential parent, the court generally must find a change in circumstances since the last order and that the change would serve the child’s best interest. Parenting time schedules can usually be adjusted on a best-interest showing. Read more on our post-decree modification page. Custody also affects child support, so the two issues often move together.

Common questions

Does Ohio favor mothers in custody cases?

No. Under ORC 3109.04, Ohio courts must treat both parents as standing on equal footing when deciding custody. The judge looks at the child's best interest using factors set out in the statute, such as each parent's relationship with the child, the child's adjustment to home and school, and which parent is more likely to support the child's time with the other parent.

At what age can a child choose which parent to live with in Ohio?

There is no age at which an Ohio child gets to decide. A judge may interview the child privately in chambers and will consider the child's wishes as one best-interest factor, giving them more weight as the child matures. The final decision still belongs to the court, which weighs the child's wishes alongside every other factor in ORC 3109.04.

What is the difference between shared parenting and being the residential parent?

With a sole allocation, one parent is named residential parent and legal custodian and makes the major decisions, while the other parent has parenting time. With shared parenting, both parents keep decision-making rights and responsibilities under a court-approved plan. Shared parenting does not have to mean an equal split of time. The plan sets the schedule.

If we were never married, who has custody?

Under ORC 3109.042, an unmarried mother is the sole residential parent and legal custodian until a court issues an order that says otherwise. A father who wants custody or parenting time must first have paternity established and then ask the juvenile court for an order. Both parents stand on equal footing once that case is filed.
