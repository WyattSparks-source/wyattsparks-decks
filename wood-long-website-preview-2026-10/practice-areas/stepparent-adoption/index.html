Stepparent Adoption

In Ohio, a stepparent adopts a spouse's child through the probate court under ORC Chapter 3107. The child's other legal parent usually must consent, unless that parent went at least a year without justifiable cause without more than minimal contact or required support. A final adoption ends the other parent's rights and duties and makes the stepparent a full legal parent.

Updated October 9, 2026.

How does stepparent adoption work in Ohio?

A stepparent adoption lets you become the legal parent of your spouse’s child. In Ohio, adoptions are governed by ORC Chapter 3107 and handled by the probate court, usually in the county where you live.

The basic steps are:

  1. File a petition to adopt in probate court, with your spouse’s consent.
  2. Address the other parent’s consent. Either the other legal parent signs a consent, or you ask the court to find that consent is not required.
  3. Give notice to anyone entitled to it, including the other parent.
  4. Complete background checks and any assessment the court requires.
  5. Attend the final hearing, where the judge decides whether the adoption is in the child’s best interest.

If the court grants the adoption, it issues a final decree. Ohio then issues a new birth certificate, and the child’s name can be changed as part of the case.

Whose consent is needed for a stepparent adoption?

Under ORC 3107.06, consent is generally required from:

  • your spouse, the child’s parent you are married to
  • the child’s other legal parent
  • the child, if the child is over 12, unless the court finds that requiring it is not in the child’s best interest

If the child’s father was never legally established as the father, different rules apply. Whether he must consent can depend on whether he established paternity or registered with Ohio’s putative father registry.

When is the other parent’s consent not required?

The most common exception is in ORC 3107.07(A). The other parent’s consent is not required if the court finds, after notice and a hearing, that the parent failed without justifiable cause to do either of these for at least one year immediately before the petition was filed:

  • have more than minimal (de minimis) contact with the child, or
  • provide maintenance and support as required by law or court order.

A few points matter a great deal in these cases:

  • Either one is enough. A lack of contact or a lack of required support for the full year can be sufficient on its own.
  • The year is the year right before filing. Timing your petition carefully matters.
  • “Justifiable cause” is a real defense. If the custodial parent blocked contact, or the other parent had a genuine inability to pay, the court may find the failure was justified.
  • You carry the burden. You must prove the failure and the lack of justifiable cause by clear and convincing evidence.

If the other parent objects and the court finds consent is required, the adoption cannot go forward without it.

Is a home study required for a stepparent adoption in Ohio?

Usually not a full one. Most adoptions in Ohio require a home study and a prefinalization assessment by a licensed assessor. For stepparent adoptions, ORC 3107.12 generally does not require the prefinalization assessment unless the court orders one in the child’s best interest. Probate courts commonly still require criminal background checks and may have their own local forms, so check the rules of the county where you file.

What does adoption do to the other parent’s rights?

A final adoption decree is permanent. Under ORC 3107.15, the decree:

  • ends the other biological parent’s legal rights and responsibilities toward the child, including parenting time and the duty to pay support going forward
  • makes you the child’s legal parent for all purposes, just as if the child had been born to you, including inheritance
  • leaves your spouse’s parental rights unchanged

Adoption can also affect the other parent’s relatives, including visitation by the other parent’s family. See our grandparent rights page. A final adoption can be challenged only in rare cases and within strict time limits, so it is important to be sure before filing.

If you later divorce your spouse, you remain the child’s legal parent, and child custody and support would be decided the same way as for any other parent.

Common questions

Can I adopt my stepchild without the other parent's consent in Ohio?

Sometimes. Under ORC 3107.07(A), consent is not required if the probate court finds that the other parent, without justifiable cause, failed to have more than minimal contact with the child or failed to provide required support for at least one year immediately before the petition was filed. You must prove this by clear and convincing evidence, and the other parent gets notice and a hearing.

Which court handles stepparent adoptions in Ohio?

Adoptions in Ohio are handled by the probate court, not the domestic relations or juvenile court. You generally file in the probate court of the county where you live. The probate court decides whether the other parent's consent is required, reviews any background checks or assessments, and holds the final hearing before granting the adoption.

Does a stepparent adoption end the other parent's child support?

A final adoption decree ends the other biological parent's parental rights and responsibilities, which includes the duty to pay child support going forward. The stepparent then takes on a full legal duty to support the child. Whether unpaid support from before the adoption can still be collected is a separate question to raise with a lawyer before you file.

Does my stepchild have to agree to the adoption?

If the child is over 12, Ohio generally requires the child's consent to the adoption under ORC 3107.06, unless the court finds that requiring it is not in the child's best interest. Younger children do not give formal consent, but the court may still consider their views when deciding whether the adoption is in their best interest.
