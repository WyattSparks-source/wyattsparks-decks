Property Division

In an Ohio divorce or dissolution, the court first decides which property is marital and which is separate under ORC 3105.171. Separate property, such as what you owned before marriage or inherited, generally stays with you. Marital property and debts are divided equally unless an equal split would be unfair, in which case the court divides them in the way it finds equitable.

Updated October 9, 2026.

How is property divided in an Ohio divorce?

Ohio courts divide property in two steps under ORC 3105.171. First, the court decides whether each asset and debt is marital or separate. Second, it gives each spouse their separate property and divides the marital property.

The starting point for marital property is an equal division. If an equal split would be inequitable, the court divides it equitably, meaning in the way it finds fair. To decide, the court looks at factors listed in the statute, including the length of the marriage, each spouse’s assets and debts, the wish to award the family home to the spouse with custody of the children, how liquid the property is, tax consequences, the costs of selling an asset, and any agreement the spouses made. The same rules apply whether the case is a divorce or a dissolution, though in a dissolution you and your spouse set the terms yourselves.

What counts as marital property in Ohio?

Marital property is generally anything either spouse acquired during the marriage, no matter whose name is on it. That includes wages saved, homes, vehicles, retirement contributions, and the increase in value of separate property that came from either spouse’s work, money or effort during the marriage.

“During the marriage” usually runs from the wedding date to the date of the final divorce hearing. The court can choose a different date, such as the date the spouses separated, if that would be more fair.

What counts as separate property?

Separate property stays with the spouse who owns it. Under ORC 3105.171, it includes:

  • Property you owned before the marriage
  • An inheritance received by one spouse during the marriage
  • A gift made to one spouse only
  • Passive income and appreciation on separate property (growth that did not come from either spouse’s work or marital money)
  • Property acquired after a decree of legal separation
  • Compensation for a personal injury, except the part meant to cover lost marital earnings or marital expenses
  • Property excluded by a valid prenuptial agreement

Mixing separate and marital property does not automatically make it marital. Separate property keeps its character as long as it can be traced. The spouse claiming separate property has to show where it came from, so old account statements, closing documents and inheritance records matter.

How are retirement accounts and pensions divided?

The part of a retirement account or pension earned during the marriage is marital property. The part earned before the marriage is generally separate. Dividing the marital share usually requires a separate court order called a qualified domestic relations order (QDRO), which instructs the plan administrator to pay the other spouse their share. Some plans, including many public pensions and military retirement, use their own type of order.

Details matter here: survivor benefits, loans against the account, investment gains between the divorce and the transfer, and tax treatment all depend on how the order is written.

What happens to the house?

The marital home is usually handled in one of three ways: one spouse keeps it and buys out the other spouse’s share of the equity, the house is sold and the proceeds are divided, or, less often, the spouses keep it jointly for a set time, such as until children finish school. A spouse keeping the home typically needs to refinance the mortgage to remove the other spouse’s name. If the house was owned before the marriage, part of its equity may be separate property.

How are debts and business interests handled?

Marital debts are divided the same way as marital assets. Remember that your divorce decree does not change your contract with a lender, so joint debts should be paid off, refinanced or closed where possible.

A business started or grown during the marriage is often one of the largest marital assets. The court must place a value on it, which often involves a business appraiser reviewing financial records. The spouse who runs the business usually keeps it, and the other spouse receives other property or a payment to balance the division.

What if my spouse hides or wastes money?

Ohio law lets the court respond to financial misconduct, such as hiding assets, dissipating money, or fraudulently transferring property. Under ORC 3105.171, the court may give the other spouse a larger share of marital property or a distributive award to make up for it. Once a property division is final, the court generally cannot modify it later, so it is important to get it right the first time.

Common questions

Does Ohio split everything 50/50 in a divorce?

Ohio starts with an equal division of marital property, but not of everything. Under ORC 3105.171, separate property, such as assets owned before marriage, inheritances and gifts to one spouse, is generally returned to its owner. Marital property is divided equally unless the court finds an equal split would be inequitable, in which case it divides the property in a way it finds fair after weighing the statutory factors.

Is my inheritance marital property in Ohio?

An inheritance received by one spouse during the marriage is separate property under ORC 3105.171. It can lose that status if it is mixed with marital money so thoroughly that it can no longer be traced, for example, deposited in a joint account and spent on shared expenses. Keeping records that show where the money came from and where it went is the main way to protect it.

What is a QDRO?

A QDRO, or qualified domestic relations order, is a separate court order that tells a retirement plan administrator how to divide a 401(k), pension or similar plan between spouses. The divorce decree says how the account is divided; the QDRO carries it out. Without one, many plans will not pay the former spouse their share. Some plans, such as government pensions, use a differently named order.

What happens to our debts in an Ohio divorce?

The court divides marital debts along with marital assets, and the same equal-unless-inequitable rule applies. Keep in mind that the divorce decree binds you and your spouse, not your creditors. If both names are on a loan or card and your former spouse stops paying, the lender can still pursue you. Refinancing or closing joint accounts helps limit that risk.
