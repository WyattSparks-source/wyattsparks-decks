Ohio family law

Child Support

Ohio sets child support with a guideline worksheet under ORC Chapter 3119 that combines both parents' incomes and divides the cost of raising the child between them. Support usually lasts until 18, or until 19 if the child is still in high school, and can be changed when the recalculated amount differs by more than 10%.

Updated October 9, 2026.

How is child support calculated in Ohio?

Ohio calculates child support with a guideline worksheet set out in ORC Chapter 3119. The worksheet uses an income shares model. The idea is simple: the child should receive about the same share of parental income the child would have received if the parents lived together.

The basic steps are:

  1. Find each parent’s annual gross income, including wages, self-employment income, bonuses and certain other sources.
  2. Subtract allowed adjustments, such as support paid for other children.
  3. Add the two incomes to get a combined income.
  4. Look up the basic child support obligation for that combined income and number of children on Ohio’s schedule.
  5. Divide that obligation by each parent’s percentage of the combined income.

The worksheet then accounts for health insurance, cash medical support, work-related child care costs and parenting time. If a parent is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed, the court can assign that parent potential income based on what they could earn.

Can the court order a different amount than the worksheet?

Yes. The guideline amount is presumed correct, but under ORC 3119.22 a court can deviate from it if the guideline amount would be unjust or inappropriate and not in the child’s best interest. The court must explain its reasons in writing.

ORC 3119.23 lists factors the court may consider, including:

  • extended parenting time or extraordinary costs tied to parenting time
  • a child’s special or extraordinary needs
  • each parent’s other court-ordered obligations
  • significant in-kind contributions from a parent
  • the standard of living the child would have had if the parents stayed together
  • a large disparity in the parents’ incomes or households

Deviation is not automatic. You have to ask for it and show why it fits.

Does parenting time reduce child support?

It can. The worksheet includes a parenting time adjustment that reduces the paying parent’s obligation by 10% when the order gives that parent 90 or more overnights a year. Parents with more time than that, including equal time under a shared parenting plan, may also ask the court for a deviation.

How long does child support last in Ohio?

Under ORC 3119.86, child support usually lasts until the child turns 18. If the child is still attending an accredited high school full time at 18, support continues until graduation, but not beyond age 19 unless the order says otherwise. Support can continue past these ages for a child with a mental or physical disability that began before 18 and keeps the child from supporting themselves.

Ohio courts generally cannot order a parent to pay college costs unless the parents agree to it.

How do I change a child support order?

You can ask for a change in one of two places:

  • Administrative review through the CSEA. The county child support enforcement agency (CSEA) can review an order, run a new worksheet and recommend an adjusted amount. Either parent may object and ask for a court hearing.
  • A motion in court. You can file a motion to modify with the court that issued the order, usually the domestic relations court or juvenile court.

Under ORC 3119.79, if the recalculated guideline amount is more than 10% higher or lower than the current order, that difference counts as a substantial change of circumstances. Common reasons include a job change, a large raise or loss of income, a change in health insurance costs, or a change in custody. See our post-decree modification page for more.

A change usually can’t reach back earlier than the date the motion or review request was filed, so it helps not to wait.

Who handles child support: the court or the CSEA?

Both play a role. In a divorce, dissolution or custody case, the court sets support as part of the case. If the parents were never married, support may be set by the juvenile court or through an administrative order from the CSEA, often after paternity is established. In either path, the CSEA usually collects and distributes payments and handles enforcement, such as wage withholding.

Support is tied closely to child custody, because the parenting schedule affects the worksheet.

Common questions

How is child support calculated in Ohio?

Ohio uses an income shares model. The court or child support agency adds both parents' annual incomes, looks up the basic support obligation for that combined income and number of children on the state schedule, and divides it by each parent's share of the total. Adjustments are made for health insurance, child care, other children and parenting time. The result is the guideline amount.

When does child support end in Ohio?

Under ORC 3119.86, support generally continues until the child turns 18. If the child is still attending an accredited high school on a full-time basis, support continues until graduation but not past age 19, unless the order says otherwise. Support can continue longer for a child who cannot support themselves because of a mental or physical disability that began before age 18.

Can I stop paying child support if I don't get to see my child?

No. In Ohio, child support and parenting time are separate obligations. If you stop paying, you can build up arrears and face enforcement such as wage withholding or license suspension. If the other parent is denying your court-ordered parenting time, the right step is to file a motion asking the court to enforce the parenting time order.

How do I change my child support order?

You can ask the county child support enforcement agency for an administrative review or file a motion with the court that issued the order. Under ORC 3119.79, if a new guideline calculation is more than 10% higher or lower than the current amount, that difference counts as a substantial change of circumstances that supports a modification.
