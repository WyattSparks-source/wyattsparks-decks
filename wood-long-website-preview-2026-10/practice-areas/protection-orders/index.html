Ohio family law

Protection Orders

An Ohio domestic violence civil protection order is a court order that tells a family or household member to stay away and stop any abuse. You can ask for a temporary ex parte order on the same day you file, and the court then holds a full hearing where both sides can be heard. A final order can last up to five years.

What is a domestic violence civil protection order in Ohio?

A domestic violence civil protection order (often called a DVCPO or CPO) is a civil court order that protects you from a family or household member who has harmed you or put you in fear of harm. It is created by ORC 3113.31.

“Family or household member” includes a current or former spouse, someone you live or lived with as a spouse, a parent or child, other relatives who live with you, and the other parent of your child. Ohio law also covers people in a dating relationship. In Franklin County, these petitions are filed with the Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations. If the person you need protection from is under 18, the case goes to the Juvenile Court instead.

A protection order case is separate from any criminal charges. You do not need a police report to file, and you can file whether or not you are also in a divorce or custody case.

How fast can I get a protection order?

You can ask for a temporary order on the same day you file. When you file a petition and request an ex parte order, the court must hold a short hearing that day. “Ex parte” means the other person is not there. If the judge or magistrate finds it is needed to protect you, a temporary order takes effect once issued.

The court then schedules a full hearing, usually within seven or ten court days, depending on what the temporary order includes. The respondent is served with the petition and the temporary order and has a chance to be heard. Either side may ask to move the hearing date, and the temporary order generally stays in place until the hearing.

What can a protection order include?

A protection order can be shaped to fit your situation. Depending on the facts, the court can:

  • Order the respondent not to abuse, contact, or come near you and other protected family members
  • Order the respondent to stay away from your home, work, school, or other places you go
  • Give you sole use of a shared home for a period of time
  • Set temporary custody, parenting time, and support for your children
  • Order the respondent not to have firearms or other deadly weapons
  • Order counseling or other terms the court finds fair

A final order after the full hearing can last up to five years, and it can be renewed. Only the respondent can be charged with violating the order. Violating it is a crime under ORC 2919.27, so police can arrest for a violation.

What is a civil stalking protection order?

A civil stalking protection order protects you from someone who is not a family or household member, such as a neighbor, coworker, or acquaintance. It is created by ORC 2903.214. You generally need to show a pattern of conduct that caused you to believe you would be physically harmed or that caused you mental distress, which is the offense of menacing by stalking. It can also be based on a sexually oriented offense.

The process is similar: a same-day or next-court-day ex parte hearing, a full hearing, and an order that can last up to five years. In Franklin County, these petitions are filed in the Court of Common Pleas.

How is a protection order different from a restraining order in a divorce?

A protection order is about safety. A divorce restraining order is about keeping things stable while a case is open. When a divorce or legal separation is filed in Ohio, the court often issues standard restraining orders under Ohio Civil Rule 75 that keep both spouses from hiding assets, canceling insurance, or harassing each other.

Those divorce orders end when the divorce ends, and violating them is handled as contempt of court. A civil protection order is its own case, can last years after a divorce, and violating it is a crime. If you are going through a divorce and safety is a concern, you may need both.

What if I have been served with a petition?

If you are the respondent, take the order seriously and follow it exactly from the moment you are served, even if you believe the claims are untrue. Do not contact the person who filed, including through friends, family, or social media. Contact can lead to arrest, even if the other person reaches out first.

At the full hearing, you have the right to tell your side, bring documents and witnesses, and question the petitioner’s witnesses. Some cases end with a consent agreement instead of a contested hearing. A protection order can affect your housing, your time with your children, your job, and your right to have firearms, so it is worth preparing carefully. If you share children, the order may also affect a later custody case.

Common questions

Can I get a protection order the same day I file in Ohio?

Often, yes. If you ask for an ex parte order when you file a domestic violence petition, Ohio law requires the court to hold a short hearing that same day. If the judge or magistrate finds it is needed, a temporary order can be issued right away. The other person does not need to be present for this first step. The order lasts until the full hearing.

How long does a civil protection order last in Ohio?

After a full hearing, a domestic violence civil protection order or a civil stalking protection order can last up to five years. The court sets the end date in the order itself. Before an order expires, the protected person can ask the court to renew it. A temporary ex parte order lasts only until the full hearing is held, unless the hearing is continued.

Is a protection order the same as a restraining order in a divorce?

No. A restraining order in a divorce is usually a standard order that keeps both spouses from hiding money, selling property or harassing each other while the case is open. A civil protection order is a separate case focused on safety. It can last years after a divorce ends, and breaking it is a crime, not just contempt of court.

I was served with a protection order petition. What should I do?

Read the order carefully and follow every term right away, even if you believe the petition is untrue. Do not contact the person who filed, even to apologize or explain. Write down the full hearing date and plan to attend. You have the right to present your side, bring evidence and question witnesses. Talking with a lawyer before the hearing can help you prepare.
